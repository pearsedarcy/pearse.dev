I built my first real website because I was tired of typing the same message.

## The event guy

I raced downhill skateboards around Europe every summer from 2014. Somewhere along the way I became the event guy. If you wanted to know what was on that year, where it was, and whether it was worth the drive, you asked me.

Usually in a DM. I would reply with a rushed, rough calendar typed from memory, and then type it again for the next person. Several times a week.

## The sites that went dark

That used to be somebody else's job. Freerides.org had listed events for years, and the International Downhill Federation's website had a calendar of its own. By the time I was answering DMs, Freerides.org had been offline for a few years and the IDF site had not been touched since 2020. Social media still had events, but only if you already followed the organiser, which is no help to someone looking for their first race abroad. [VERIFY: the raw essay spells it both Freerides.org and Freeride.org; which is right?]

A system that had worked almost perfectly for my whole youth had stopped working.

## Building it for myself

So I built one. I was injured at the time and out of work, and I had always been curious about how websites were made. Both of the old sites ran on WordPress, so that is what I used. I had been tinkering with WordPress for a shop idea that never went anywhere; I dropped it and put everything into a calendar.

I called it Skatedownhills. When someone asked what was on, I sent the link.

Honestly, I built it for me. Planning a summer of races was always one of my favourite parts of the year. I assumed I would be the main user, with a few friends checking in now and then.

## Strangers saying thanks

The first summer it was live, people I was meeting for the first time thanked me for it. Far more riders had used it to plan their season than I had imagined. A few mentioned it in passing without knowing they were talking to the person who built it.

That landed harder than I expected. Racing is a fairly selfish pursuit: you travel, you take what you came for, you go home. This was the first thing I had made that gave something back to the sport.

## The knee, and the pivot

In 2021 I hurt my knee snowboarding. It changed what I could do on a board, and it shook my confidence. Around the same time, the European Championship I had spent years chasing stopped existing.

Watching the riders who were about to become the best in the world, I could see my role in the scene changing. Either I changed with it or I got left behind.

Skatedownhills pointed at the answer. The thing from those years I was proudest of was not a result. It was a website.

## Back to study, and the rebuild

In November 2023 I went back to study software development, a full-stack diploma.

My first Flask app was Skatedownhills again, rebuilt in Python. Then, for my final diploma project, I rebuilt it a third time, from scratch, in Django: event management, rider profiles, calendar views with filtering and maps, and email notifications. The goal was the one I had written down when I started: a reliable place on the internet where people can find downhill skateboarding events, and hopefully go to them.

Building the same thing three times taught me more than three different projects would have. WordPress taught me to ship. Flask showed me what a framework does for you by making me do it myself. Django handed me models, an admin and accounts, and left me to spend my time on the calendar. The [case study](/work/skatedownhills/) has the details.

## Where it went

While I was rebuilding it, I took my first freelance job, a website for someone I had never met, and a part-time job with a company that runs hands-on surgical training. A few months in, I realised I could put the new skill to work on the new job. That turned into the platform work I do now, starting with [MTS Hub](/work/mts-hub/).

My days went from constant uncertainty to consistency and structure, something I had never really had. I have stood on a skateboard for about two hours in the past year, and I have not missed it once. I ride downhill mountain bikes now, with a new group of friends.

It all started with a message I was tired of typing.
